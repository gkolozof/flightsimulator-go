A flight school simulator, not a computer game

The machines on this site are professional Cessna 172 and Cessna 152 training devices for flight schools and aero clubs. They are not the PC game that uses a similar name. The student sits in a full-size cabin, holds a real yoke and looks at a wide visual system. The instructor starts the session from an iPad or iPhone. There is no separate desktop computer to boot and no external controller box to configure.

A school uses the simulator for procedures, cockpit familiarisation, radio work and the first hours of handling, before the student burns fuel in the aircraft. The operating cost is about €3 per hour. There is no fuel, no engine time and no airframe wear. Schools that move a large share of early training into the simulator commonly save 30 to 92 percent against flying the same exercises in the real Cessna.

What is included

  • A full-size Cessna 172 or 152 cabin, in Big or Mini size.
  • Fully automatic start: ready to fly in about 65 seconds.
  • Control from an iPad or iPhone, with no external controller.
  • Real primer, carburettor heat and fuses, not painted switches.
  • A visual system sized for the model you choose.
  • Turnkey delivery, installation support and a usual lead time of 2 to 3 months.

Two sizes and a clear price

The Big models need about 5.2 × 4.62 metres. The Mini models fit in about 2.4 × 2.8 metres, which is a classroom or a corner of a hangar. Both lines exist as a simpler version and as a professional version. Purchase prices for the full-embedded professional units start at about €65,000 to €70,000. The ATC simulator, built for tower, approach and multi-sector training, is a separate system and starts at about €350,000. Cockpit parts, including the Magic Yoke, Garmin-style panels, a radio, a transponder and a compass, can be bought on their own if you are building a device yourself.

Who it is built for

The main buyers are flight schools, aero clubs and training organisations that want more hours on the ground without losing the feel of a Cessna. Owners also use it as a private procedure trainer in a garage or a small hall. It runs in any weather, day or night, so the timetable does not stop for wind or for a grounded aircraft.

We check the room, the power supply and the access path before the order. The unit is tested and then delivered. After installation we stay available for maintenance and remote support. If you already know the room size, write to info@virtualsky.gr with the model you prefer and the country of delivery.

What the simulator replaces, and what it does not

Simulator time does not replace every hour required by the regulations. It does remove a large part of the cost and the risk from the early lessons. Work associated with the University of Illinois reports about 40 percent fewer errors in the first real flight hours after structured simulator training. European and US authorities both treat a flight simulation training device as a normal tool for pilot training, not as a toy. Use the official pages when you design a syllabus:

  • EASA: Flight Simulation Training Devices
  • FAA: National Simulator Program

A quotation includes the chosen model, shipping to your country, installation and the first training for your instructors. You do not have to buy the parts separately: the full-embedded simulator is delivered as one machine.

Two practical examples

These are worked examples, not a promise that every school will see the same number. They use the prices and the sizes published on this site, so a buyer can see what the machine is for before asking for a quotation.

A classroom and a hangar

A school with one spare classroom of about 2.4 by 2.8 metres can take a Mini. That is enough for the compact Cessna 172 or Cessna 152. The student sits in the cabin, the instructor stands beside the door, and the visual system stays inside that footprint. A school with a small hangar bay of about 5.2 by 4.62 metres can take a Big. The Big is the same kind of trainer, with more room around the cabin. Before the order we measure the room, the power supply and the path from the door to the final position, because a machine that cannot be brought in is of no use.

In a normal morning the instructor opens the session from an iPad or iPhone. The full-embedded simulator is ready in about 65 seconds. The student practises the primer, carburettor heat, the fuses and a short radio call, then the instructor stops the lesson. There is no fuel truck, no walk-around of a cold aircraft, and the timetable does not stop because the wind is too strong for the real Cessna.

Ten early hours, on paper

Take ten hours of early procedures, the lessons a student repeats before he or she needs the real aircraft. On this simulator the operating cost is about €3 an hour, so those ten hours cost about €30 to run. There is no fuel, no engine time and no airframe wear. The same ten hours in a rented Cessna are the hours schools are trying to reduce. The exact figure depends on the local rental price and on how many hours the syllabus is allowed to move.
